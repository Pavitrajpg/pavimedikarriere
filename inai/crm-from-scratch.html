What happens when you build a CRM from scratch in 15 days.

Pavitra Muthukrishnan  ·  June 2025  ·  7 min read

The brief was simple. Step4Progress needed a CRM. They had leads coming in from multiple sources, a team spread across four countries, and no single system to track any of it. Spreadsheets were being emailed back and forth. Follow-ups were being missed. Nobody had a clear picture of what the pipeline actually looked like.

The timeline was fifteen days. Not because that was a comfortable deadline. Because that was when the next intake cycle was starting and the business needed to be ready.

This is what happened.

Day 1 to 3: Refusing to start with a tool.

The first instinct when someone asks for a CRM is to go and look at CRM tools. Salesforce. HubSpot. Zoho. Pick one, configure it, hand it over. That would have taken longer than fifteen days and produced something the team would have abandoned within a month.

Instead, days one to three were spent asking questions. What does a lead look like when it arrives? What information matters? Who needs to see what, and who should not see what? What happens between first contact and enrolment? Where are the gaps that currently let leads disappear?

By day three, there was a clear picture. The business needed role-based access because team members in India should not see UK client data. It needed a pipeline view with stage tracking. It needed a course finder so counsellors could quickly identify programmes for students. It needed call recording so conversations could be reviewed. And it needed to connect to their existing WhatsApp and Instagram channels where most leads arrived.

The tool should serve the process. The process should serve the business. Build in that order, never the other way around.

The technical decisions.

Supabase for the database. Free tier, PostgreSQL, row-level security that could enforce the India view-only restriction at the database level rather than relying on frontend code. Reliable, scalable, and something that could be handed over without depending on a proprietary vendor.

A single HTML file on GitHub Pages for the frontend. No framework, no build step, no deployment pipeline to break. One file, one URL, accessible from any browser on any device anywhere in the world. The team in Pakistan and Bangladesh did not need to install anything.

Anthropic API for the course finder. 645 partner universities. No counsellor was going to memorise those programmes. An AI-powered search built on the Anthropic API let counsellors describe what a student needed and surface matching programmes in seconds.

Twilio for call recording via Edge Functions. Every counselling call needed to be recordable and reviewable. Supabase Edge Functions handled the webhook, Twilio handled the recording, and the files were stored and linked to the student record.

What was built in 15 days
  • Role-based access control with India view-only enforcement at the database level
  • Lead pipeline with stage tracking and status updates
  • 645-university course finder with AI-powered suggestions
  • Bulk lead upload from CSV
  • Twilio call recording linked to student records
  • Student portal with animated lesson delivery
  • Meta Ads and Google Analytics 4 integration
  • Resource library with lead-gated downloads
  • Multi-admin system with Super Admin roles

What almost went wrong.

Day seven. The row-level security policies that enforce India view-only access were conflicting with the Super Admin role that Jisan in the UK needed. An admin should see everything. A staff member in India should see only India leads. The RLS policies were not distinguishing correctly between the two.

This is the kind of problem that does not show up in planning. It shows up at 11pm on a Tuesday when you are testing the access controls and realise that fixing one thing has broken something else. The solution was restructuring the policy logic to check role hierarchy rather than just region flag. It took four hours. It worked.

The honest version of building from scratch is that things break in ways you did not anticipate, and the timeline only works if you stay in it and fix them rather than escalating or replanning.

The part that was not in the brief.

While the CRM was being built, Instagram was sitting at 300 followers and posting inconsistently. That was not the brief. But it was clearly connected to the same problem: the business had no infrastructure for growth, digital or operational.

A content strategy was put together. Student stories. Destination education guides. Visa process explainers. Scholarship updates. Consistently posted, consistently engaged with. In ten days, the account went from 300 to 1,030 followers.

This is what INAI Systems actually means in practice. Not just building a database. Building the operational infrastructure that allows a business to function and grow at the same time.

A CRM that nobody trusts is worse than no CRM at all. The goal was not to build a system. The goal was to build a system that people actually used.

What fifteen days proved.

You do not need six months and a six-figure budget to have functioning business infrastructure. You need clarity on what the process actually is, decisions made quickly when the technical choices arrive, and someone who can hold the full picture in their head from the database schema to the user experience to the business outcome it is meant to serve.

Step4Progress now has a CRM that is in daily use across four countries. The India team has access to what they need and nothing they should not. The UK team has full visibility. The counsellors have a course finder that takes three seconds instead of twenty minutes. The leads are tracked. The calls are recorded. The pipeline is visible.

It took fifteen days. It works.

If you are running a business on spreadsheets and the next intake cycle is coming, the timeline is shorter than you think and longer than zero. The conversation to have is now.
